The Pastafarian Calendar is a non-intuitive calendar with a deliberately unusual structure: a day’s date depends not only on the day being described, but also on the day from which the calculation is performed.

It has years, cutlets, and months – but the years are not fixed in length, cutlets and months divide the year in different ways, months can disappear and return over the course of a year, and there are no canonical weeks.

This page explains what the calendar is, how to read a date, and what its structure means in practice. The mathematical and cryptographic implementation details are available in the technical documentation, but you do not need them in order to understand the calendar.

What does a Pastafarian date look like?

A Pastafarian date has exactly five parts:

  1. year since the creation of the world;
  2. cutlet name;
  3. day in the cutlet;
  4. month name;
  5. day in the month.

In other words, a complete date says which year the day falls in, which cutlet it falls in and its position within that cutlet, and which month it belongs to and which occurrence of that month it represents.

The day of working, the observer’s location, and other technical details may be essential for calculating the date, but they are not a sixth, seventh, or eighth part of the date itself.

The display layout does not change the number of parts either. If the site prints the five pieces of information on three lines, there are still five pieces of information; a line break is a typographic operation, not the birth of a new calendar field.

Why can the same day get a different date?

Every calculation involves two days:

  • day of working – the day from which the calculation is performed;
  • day in question – the day whose date you want to know.

If the day of working is denoted by c and the day in question by t, the date is F(c,t), not F(t).

That means the same chronological day can receive a different Pastafarian representation when the day of working changes. The day itself does not move; only its Pastafarian description changes.

When c=t, the day is always in Year 5000. Year 5000 is not a fixed historical period: it is rebuilt relative to the day of working, so additional days around the day of working can belong to it as well.

For example, if a particular day is its own day of working, it is in Year 5000. If the calculation is performed again the next day, with tomorrow now serving as the new day of working, tomorrow is also in Year 5000. These are not two contradictory claims about the same “historical year”; each calculation selects a new base year.

Years numbered above 5000 point toward the future relative to the day of working, while years numbered below 5000 point toward the past. Year 0 exists too, followed by years with negative numbers.

How is a year structured?

A Pastafarian year can contain between 252 and 5,778 days. Both endpoints are included: a 252-day year is valid, and so is a 5,778-day year; a 251-day or 5,779-day year is not valid under the current canonical rules.

Those bounds are part of the calendar. The year does not try to match a solar year, a lunar year, a season, a school year, or the patience of anyone waiting for the next New Year.

Each year is organized according to two different systems:

  • 6–17 cutlets. Each cutlet is a contiguous span of days and is at least 42 days long.
  • 3–47 structural months. Each month has 4–123 days belonging to it, but those days do not have to be contiguous.

The calendar has no canonical week system. Rows and columns shown on the site are only a display method; the fact that two days appear next to each other does not make them members of the same “Pastafarian week.”

What does a “woven month” mean?

A cutlet is sequential: if today is day 250 of a cutlet and tomorrow is still in the same cutlet, tomorrow is day 251.

A month works differently. Day 18 of a particular month is the 18th time that month appears during the year. It does not have to come one day after day 17.

Month A – day 14
Month B – day 9
Month A – day 15

A month can therefore stretch across a large part of the year, pass through several cutlets, and be woven together with other months. A single cutlet can likewise contain days belonging to many different months.

A month’s length is the total number of days that belong to it, not the number of days elapsed between its first and last appearances. A 104-day month can therefore span thousands of days chronologically.

Why does day 15 come after day 14?

Because 15 is the next integer after 14. The fact that a day from another month appears between those two appearances does not change the arithmetic: the first month has already appeared 14 times; its next appearance is its 15th appearance, so its number is 15.

In the same way, if four days from other months appear after day 15 of that month, the next appearance of the original month is still day 16 of that month. The four intervening days count chronologically in the calendar, but they do not belong to that month, so they do not increase its day counter. This is exactly why “the next day in the month” and “tomorrow” are two different concepts.

It also follows that the end of a month is not necessarily close in time. A month may be on day 119 out of 120, while its day 120 may still appear much later.

Cutlet and month names

The calendar has 17 cutlet names and 47 month names. Within a year, a name does not appear twice in the same family. Not every name has to appear in every year: a year may use only part of the catalog.

The lists show the names only. Selecting a name or the disclosure icon next to it reveals its precise meaning and any relevant canonical notes. A name is not a code for the unit’s length and does not predict what will happen in it.

When does the day change?

A local Pastafarian day does not change at midnight. Its boundary is determined by the lower transit of the center of Venus across the local meridian: the moment when the center of Venus crosses the observer’s local meridian on the side below the horizon.

The observer’s location therefore matters. Two people in different places can, at the same physical instant, belong to two different local Pastafarian days. When you travel to another city, you do not keep using the day boundary of the previous city.

The boundary depends on calculating the orbit of Venus, not on whether the observer can actually see it. Clouds, walls, or the fact that Venus is below the horizon do not stop its orbit.

The calendar’s discrete algorithm is defined precisely: the same inputs, passed through the same rules, return the same result. By contrast, converting a physical instant into a Pastafarian day currently uses the implementation’s astronomical model; the numerical details of that model are not yet fully specified by the canon.

The calendar’s outstanding advantages

  • A date you can calculate again and again: the same day in the past can receive a different Pastafarian date tomorrow, because the day of working has advanced too. There is no need to settle for an old date that remains useful over time.
  • Roomy years: a year can reach 5,778 days, so anyone waiting for next year may enjoy a much longer wait than usual.
  • Months that demand attention: knowing that today is day 119 of a month does not mean its day 120 will occur tomorrow, next week, or even soon. The next occurrence has to be calculated.
  • Geographic sensitivity: the same instant can belong to different local Pastafarian days in different places. Traveling to another city therefore adds one more detail worth remembering when coordinating plans.
  • Printed calendars never become complacent: a calendar prepared in advance may stop representing the correct calculation after the day of working changes, so there is no danger that anyone will make do with the same sheet of paper for years.
  • A useful use of computing power: instead of settling for a simple table that can be understood at a glance, the calendar gives a computer the opportunity to perform a real calculation every time an answer is wanted.
  • It has days: the calendar deals with days. This is a feature it shares with every calendar whatsoever, ensuring that the user will not be forced to manage a calendar with no days in it.
  • The days appear in order: an earlier day comes before a later day. This is a fundamental achievement of calendars, and here it is available at no additional charge.
  • You can use it to state dates: the calendar makes it possible to assign a calendrical description to a day. Admittedly, this is exactly what calendars are designed to do, but there is no reason not to mention a useful feature when one exists.

And all of the above comes together within a single calendar, as happens when several features belong to the same calendar.

What does this mean in practice?

A printed calendar ages badly. Changing the day of working can change the boundaries of years, cutlets, and months. An almanac calculated today is not necessarily the correct almanac for tomorrow.

An event remains the same event. A meeting, birth, or historical event should be anchored to a stable chronological day or instant; a Pastafarian date can then be displayed for it according to the day of working and the observer’s location. Changing the label does not move the event through time.

A Pastafarian birthday is not a “once a year” rule. To find the next occurrence of the same month name and day in the month, or the same cutlet name and day in the cutlet, you have to search the calendar. A nearby year may not contain the required name at all, or it may contain a unit too short to reach the desired day number.

An all-day event is not necessarily midnight to midnight. If the event is defined by a local Pastafarian day, its boundary is the local Venus boundary. Naively exporting it to a civil calendar as a midnight-to-midnight event can change its meaning.

Foundation Day and Day of Tablets

The calendar has two important fixed anchors:

Foundation Day
December 22, 41,222 BCE in the proleptic Gregorian calendar.
Day of Tablets
June 15, 763 BCE in the proleptic Julian calendar, which is June 7, 763 BCE in the proleptic Gregorian calendar.

The distance between them is 14,777,149 days. Foundation Day is a computational anchor; it is not “the first day of time,” and the calendar continues before it.

Day of Tablets is traditionally associated with the delivery of the tablets and, in the historical calculation, is identified with the day of the solar eclipse in the time of Bur-Sagale. Both anchors remain fixed even when the day of working changes.

A few simple mathematical facts

There are 47 month names, and each month can reach at most day 123. There are therefore 5,781 possible combinations of the form “month name + day in month.”

A year can contain at most 5,778 days, and each day realizes one such combination. Therefore, every year is missing at least three of the possible combinations. Even the longest year does not have enough days to realize them all.

When the day of working and the year are known, a complete pair of “cutlet name + day in cutlet” or “month name + day in month” points to at most one day within that year. A complete Pastafarian date, together with the day of working, therefore identifies the day in question unambiguously.

By contrast, without knowing the day of working, a complete Pastafarian date does not necessarily identify a unique point on the timeline by itself. The same five-part date can occur in different calculation contexts.

What have computational studies found about the calendar?

In addition to checking the rules themselves, large computational studies have been carried out on the calendar. The following figures are empirical findings from a sample, not canonical laws.

In a structural atlas built from 4,096 days of working and covering 86,016 year structures:

  • the mean year length in the sample was about 4,275 days, with a median of 4,343 days;
  • the mean number of cutlets per year was about 7.27;
  • the mean number of structural months was about 41.1;
  • a month contained about 104 days belonging to it on average, but typically stretched across almost the entire year;
  • 97.482% of contiguous month segments were only one day long;
  • the measured probability that two adjacent chronological days belonged to the same month was only about 2.998%.

In a separate study of “day-year” recurrences, which examined 4,096 self-dates in each direction:

  • a repeat match for month name + day in month appeared at a median distance of just one Pastafarian year; 77.56% of matches were in the adjacent year;
  • a repeat match for cutlet name + day in cutlet was much less predictable: the median was three Pastafarian years, but the mean was affected by a very long tail;
  • the sample included an extreme case in which the first future recurrence of “Akkad 3063” was 51,954 Pastafarian years away.

These findings are useful for understanding what the calendar tends to do. They do not turn an average into a law: the fact that the average year in the sample had a certain length does not require any particular year to be close to that average, and a result found in all 4,096 cases is not, by itself, a mathematical proof for every possible input.

How is the calendar calculated?

Behind the date is a precisely defined discrete algorithm. From the day of working and the day in question it produces a series of counts, passes them through the mixing mechanism known as the Sauce, creates gates, selects the boundaries of the year and cutlets, selects names, creates months, and finally weaves the month days throughout the year.

The exact details include large numbers, drops, bowls, stirrings, seals, and combinatorial selection mechanisms. They matter for implementation and formal verification, but they are not required in order to understand what the date means, so they are not set out here.

Alongside the algorithm there is a fast engine called the Pastafarian Calendar Seer. The Seer is designed to calculate quickly; it is not the source of authority, and it is not itself a standard implementation of every stage of the algorithm. If one of its results conflicts with the result required by the canon, the Seer is the one that is wrong.

About the Monster

In Pastafarian lore, the Flying Spaghetti Monster is the being that created the world and most of the things in it. His body is made of noodles and meatballs, and he can fly, pass through ordinary matter, and remain invisible whenever that is convenient.

The Monster can create matter, living creatures, celestial bodies, and highly complex mechanisms. That ability does not require him to prepare an orderly plan before starting work. He often begins with one thing, moves on to another, discovers that the earlier thing needs fixing, and decides whether to fix it. Sometimes he does.

This image of creation as something built layer upon layer, with repairs, exceptions, and rules left in place, is also the literary background of the calendar. Unlike the story, the calculation itself is not improvised: when the inputs are given, the algorithm returns a single defined answer.

Pastafarian lore extends far beyond the calendar and includes, among other things, pirates, carbohydrates, prayer, “I’d Really Rather You Didn’t,” and other traditions.

What is canonical and what is only explanatory?

This page is an explanatory page. It does not create a new rule merely because a sentence appears here.

Canonical content is determined by the canonical corpus and the rules adopted within it. Canonical editions of the scroll are not automatically arranged in a hierarchy, and a software implementation does not become a source of authority merely because it is fast or widely used.

A standard implementation carries out the canonical algorithm through all of its stages. Computational research can describe a phenomenon, find a pattern, or prove a theorem about the algorithm; when something is a sample finding rather than a proven rule, that is stated explicitly.

In short

The Pastafarian Calendar is a non-intuitive calendar in which the date depends both on the day being asked about and on the day from which the question is asked.

Year 5000 is built around the day of working. Cutlets are contiguous spans. Months are groups of days that can be woven together. There are no canonical weeks. The local day changes according to Venus, not at midnight.

Behind the unusual structure is a precise algorithm; around it is a Pastafarian story about a spaghetti monster, creation piled up in layers, and an unreasonable amount of carbohydrates.

When the day of working, the day in question, and the location context are given, there is one answer.
