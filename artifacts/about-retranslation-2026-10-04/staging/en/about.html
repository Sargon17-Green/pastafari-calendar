Pastafari Calendar is a deliberately non-intuitive calendar with an intentionally unusual structure: the date of a day does not depend only on the day being described, but also on the day from which the calculation is performed.

It has years, cutlets, and months — but the years are not fixed-length, the cutlets and months are not the same kind of division, the months can disappear and return during the year, and there are no canonical weeks.

This page is meant to explain what the calendar is, how to read a date, and what the practical meaning of its structure is. The detailed mathematical and cryptographic implementation details are in the technical documentation, but they are not a prerequisite for understanding the calendar.

What does a Pastafari date look like?

A Pastafari date has exactly five parts:

  1. Year from the Creation of the World;
  2. Cutlet name;
  3. Day in the cutlet;
  4. Month name;
  5. Day in the month.

In other words, a full date tells you in which year the day is found, in which cutlet it sits and where it stands within it, and which month it belongs to and what occurrence number it has in that month.

The actual transaction day, the observer’s location, and other technical details can be essential to calculate the date, but they are not a sixth, seventh, or eighth part of the date itself.

Even the display form does not change the number of parts. If the site prints the five data points across three lines, it is still five data points; a line break is typographic behavior, not the birth of a new calendrical field.

Why can the same day have a different date?

In every calculation there are two days:

  • Day of working — the day from which the calculation is performed;
  • Queried day — the day whose date you want to know.

If the day of working is marked as c and the queried day as t, then the date is F(c,t), not F(t).

Therefore, the same chronological day can receive a different Pastafari representation when the day of working changes. The day itself does not move; only its Pastafari description changes.

When c=t, the day is always in year 5000. Year 5000 is not a fixed historical period: it is rebuilt relative to the day of working, so additional days around the day of working may also belong to it.

For example: if a certain day is the day of working for itself, it is in year 5000. If the next day is calculated again when the next day becomes the new day of working, that next day is also in year 5000. There is no contradiction here about the same “historical year”; each calculation selects a new base year.

Years numbered greater than 5000 lie in the future relative to the day of working, while years numbered less than 5000 lie in the past. There is also year 0, followed by years with negative numbers.

How is a year built?

A Pastafari year can contain between 252 and 5,778 days. Both extremes are included: a 252-day year is valid, and a 5,778-day year is valid; a 251-day or 5,779-day year is not valid under the currently canonical rules.

These limits are part of the calendar. The year does not try to conform to the solar year, lunar year, season, school year, or the patience of anyone waiting for next New Year.

Every year is divided into two different systems:

  • 6–17 cutlets. Every cutlet is a continuous segment of days, at least 42 days long.
  • 3–47 structural months. Each month has 4–123 assigned days, but those days are not required to be contiguous.

There is no canonical week system in the calendar. The rows and columns that appear on the site are a display convention only; the fact that two days appear next to each other does not make them members of the same “Pastafari week.”

What does “woven month” mean?

A cutlet is a sequence: if today is day 250 in the cutlet and tomorrow is still in the same cutlet, tomorrow is day 251.

A month works differently. Day 18 in a certain month is the 18th time that this month appears during the year. It does not have to fall one day after day 17.

Month A — day 14
Month B — day 9
Month A — day 15

Therefore a month can span a large part of the year, pass through several cutlets, and be woven through other months. One cutlet can, in the same way, contain days belonging to many months.

A month’s length is the total number of days assigned to it, not the number of days that have passed since its most recent first occurrence. So a month of 104 days may thus stretch chronologically across thousands of days.

Why does day 15 come after day 14?

Because 15 is the next whole number after 14. The fact that four days of another month fall between two appearances of this month does not change the counting: the first month already had 14 appearances; its next appearance is its 15th appearance, and so it is numbered 15.

In the same way, if after day 15 of that month there are four days belonging to other months, the next appearance of the original month is still day 16 of that month. Those four days are counted chronologically in the calendar, but they do not belong to that month, and so they do not increase the counter for its days. This is exactly why “the next day in the month” and “tomorrow” are two different concepts.

From this it also follows that the end of a month is not necessarily close in time. A month can be on day 119 out of 120, and yet its day 120 may appear much later.

Cutlet and month names

The calendar has 17 cutlet names and 47 month names. Within a year, a name does not repeat twice in the same family. Not all names must appear in every year: a year can use only part of the catalog.

The lists show the names only. Clicking a name or the opening symbol next to it reveals the exact meaning and the relevant canonical notes. The name is not a code for the unit, and it is not a prediction of what will happen within it.

    The metal alloy bronze, made mainly of copper and tin. Here it is a material name, not just a color.

  • Fox

    A fox, a mammal of the dog family. The name does not indicate a particular species of fox.

The central numerical meaning is exactly 4/9; only in the lexical layer is a numerical value x satisfying |x − 4/9| ≤ e/103 also permitted, including the limit. The expression e/103 is preserved exactly and is not replaced by a decimal approximation. This permission does not alter numerators, indices, identities, or any calculation of the calendar.

  • Palgurash

    An invented sequence of syllables. It has no additional lexical meaning to be interpreted; it is the name itself.

  • Papyrus Sedge

    Papyrus sedge, the plant from which papyrus was made in antiquity.

  • Cluster

    A dense grouping or bunch of things; in Hebrew, the word is especially associated with a cluster of grapes.

  • Scorpion

    A scorpion, an arachnid with claws and a sting at its tail.

  • Ash

    The powdery residue left after burning.

  • Wheat

    The wheat plant, whose grains are used, among other things, to produce flour.

  • River

    A relatively large natural stream of water flowing in a channel.

  • Laughter

    A vocal and physical reaction typically associated with amusement, joy, or humor.

  • Akkad

    Akkad, the ancient Mesopotamian city after which the Akkadian empire and region were also named; the exact location of the city remains uncertain.

  • Horn

    Horn in the sense of the hard protrusion that grows from the heads of certain animals, not a ray of light.

  • The Empty Jar

    A jar with no contents. “Empty” describes what is inside the jar — namely, nothing — and does not cancel the jar’s existence. An empty jar can still be a complete jar, with walls, a base, and an opening; it is simply not filled with water, wine, oil, or any other substance. In the same way, the name does not mean the cutlet is empty of days: a cutlet named “The Empty Jar” has days like any other cutlet; only the jar in the name is empty. The point is not to deny the jar, but to deny its contents. This is a useful distinction because a thing may be present and still be vacant. A vessel may be structurally intact and yet be empty in precisely the way a name says; the presence of an empty vessel is not the same as the absence of the vessel itself. To say that a jar is empty is to allow the jar to remain in full formal being while refusing the material or substance that would otherwise fill it. The name therefore captures a relation between object and content, not a statement that the object vanishes or somehow ceases to be itself. In a calendar, that is a sound and consistent way to describe a category, and it is more precise than a vague claim that “nothing is there.” If the jar is empty, there are still walls, an aperture, a volume, and a recognizable container. What is absent is only the occupying matter; the jar remains whole, and the wording has not accidentally described an empty space instead of an actual object. A cutlet called “The Empty Jar” therefore enjoys all the ordinary calendaric reality of any other cutlet, while being defined chiefly by the absence of content within its symbolic vessel. It is an empty jar, not a void, and not the obliteration of a jar by a philosophical trick. The distinction is not ornamental; it is the whole point. If it were otherwise, the phrase would be a category error, and the name would end up describing an event rather than an object — which would be a very poor way to name a cutlet, even one with a grand tradition of absurd names behind it.

  • The Closed Door

    A door that is in the closed state. A closed door remains a door: it is not transformed into a wall, does not disappear, and is not necessarily locked. “Closed” means that the opening it is intended to open is currently blocked by the door itself; “locked” is an additional claim that is not part of the name. Therefore, a door that has been shut without turning a key still fits the phrase “The Closed Door” perfectly. A closed door is not an absent door, and it is not a door that has ceased to be a door by some merely rhetorical trick. It is a door whose path is presently obstructed by its own form, and that is all the name requires. If one were to make “closed” mean “locked,” the name would become needlessly specific and would lose the broad practical applicability that makes it useful. The same principle applies to a cutlet named “The Closed Door”: it is still a cutlet, still a calendaric unit, and still a valid object with all the ordinary internal geometry and counting rules. The distinguishing feature is not that it ceases to exist, but that its opening is blocked. That is the whole point. If the door were really not a door, the phrase would be a mistake in category rather than a description. Such a confusion would be only a small philosophical embarrassment, but in a naming system of 47 months and 17 cutlets, category errors are exactly the kind of thing that must be prevented from creeping in under cover of bright metaphors. In ordinary life, one does not call a door “gone” just because it is shut. One calls it closed. The same law of language and reason applies here: a door remains a door even when it impedes passage. The name is therefore not a denial of the door’s identity; it is a statement about its state. A calendar month by that name may be just as real as any other month, and just as counted, but it belongs to the class defined by its current closedness. This is a profound and highly practical distinction, which is why it is worth saying slowly: there are objects, there are conditions, and a name may refer to either. “The Closed Door” refers to the condition as applied to a door, not to a metaphysical disappearance of the door itself. This is, again, exactly the point of using a good name and a good description instead of feeding a deceptive paradox into the machinery.

When does a day change?

A local Pastafari day does not turn over at midnight. Its boundary is defined by the lower transit of Venus across the local meridian: the moment when the center of Venus crosses the local meridian on the side below the horizon.

For that reason, the observer’s location matters. Two people in different places can, at the same physical moment, belong to two different local Pastafari days. When moving to another city, one does not continue using the previous city’s day boundary.

The boundary depends on the calculation of Venus’s path, not on whether the observer actually sees it. Clouds, walls, or the fact that Venus is below the horizon do not stop its path.

The calendar’s discrete algorithm is defined precisely: the same inputs, passed through the same rules, return the same result. By contrast, the conversion from a physical instant to a Pastafari day uses the astronomical model of the implementation currently in use; the numerical astronomical profile itself is still not a closed canonical part.

Notable advantages of the calendar

  • A date that can be calculated over and over: the same day from the past can receive a different Pastafari date tomorrow because the day of working has advanced. There is no need to settle for an old date that remains “useful” over time.
  • Long, spread-out years: a year can extend to 5,778 days, so someone waiting for the next year may have a very long period of anticipation compared with the ordinary case.
  • Months that require attention: knowing that the day is day 119 in a month does not mean that day 120 will happen tomorrow, next week, or even soon. The next occurrence must be calculated.
  • Geographic sensitivity: the same moment can belong to different local Pastafari days in different places. Traveling to another city therefore adds another fact worth remembering when coordinating events.
  • Printed calendars do not get lazy: a calendar prepared in advance may cease to represent the correct calculation after the day of working changes, so there is no danger of someone relying on the same paper sheet for years.
  • Useful use of computing power: instead of settling for a simple table that can be understood at a glance, the calendar gives a computer the opportunity to perform a real calculation whenever an answer is needed.
  • There are days in it: the calendar deals in days. This is a property it shares with any calendar, and it ensures the user is not forced to manage a calendar with no days at all.
  • The days appear in order: an early day comes before a later day. This is a basic achievement of calendars, and in this case it is available without extra charge.
  • It can be used to specify dates: the calendar allows one to assign a calendrical description to a day. This is precisely what calendars are for, but there is no reason not to note a useful property when it exists.

And all of the above comes together in a single calendar, as when several features belong to the same calendar.

What does this mean in practice?

A printed calendar becomes outdated. A change in the day of working can change the boundaries of years, cutlets, and months. An almanac calculated today is not necessarily the correct almanac for tomorrow.

An event remains the same event. A meeting, birth, or historical event should be anchored to a stable chronological day or instant; only afterward can it be given a Pastafari date according to the day of working and observer location. Changing the label does not move the event in time.

A Pastafari birthday is not “once a year” in any simple sense. To find the next occurrence of a given month name and day-in-month, or the same cutlet name and day-in-cutlet, one must search the calendar. A nearby year may not contain the required name at all, or may contain it in a form too short to reach the required day number.

An all-day event is not necessarily from midnight to midnight. If the event is defined by a local Pastafari day, its boundary is the local Venus-based day boundary. A naïve export to a civil diary as a midnight-to-midnight event may change its meaning.

Foundation Day and Tablet Day

The calendar has two important fixed anchors:

Foundation Day
22 December 41,222 BCE in the Gregorian calendar extended backward.
Tablet Day
15 June 763 BCE in the Julian calendar extended backward, which is 7 June 763 BCE in the Gregorian calendar extended backward.

The distance between them is 14,777,149 days. Foundation Day is a computational anchor; it is not “the first day of time,” and the calendar continues even before it.

Tablet Day is tied to the tradition of the transmission of the tablets and the historical calculation associated with the solar eclipse in the Būr-Saggala period. Both anchors remain fixed even when the day of working changes.

A few simple mathematical facts

There are 47 month names, and each month can reach at most day 123. Therefore there are 5,781 possible pairs of the form “month name + day in month.”

A year can contain at most 5,778 days, and every day instantiates one such pair. Therefore every year is missing at least three of the possible combinations. There are not enough days even in the longest year to realize all of them.

When the day of working and the year are known, a full pair of “cutlet name + day in cutlet” or “month name + day in month” points to at most one day within that year. Therefore a full Pastafari date, together with the day of working, identifies the queried day unambiguously.

By contrast, without knowing the day of working, a full Pastafari date is not necessarily enough to identify a single point on the timeline by itself. The same five-part date can appear in different contexts of calculation.

What have computational studies found about the calendar?

In addition to testing the rules themselves, large computational studies have been performed on the calendar. The following are empirical findings from a sample, not canonical laws.

In a structural atlas built from 4,096 working days and tested on 86,016 year structures:

  • The average year length in the sample was about 4,275 days, with a median of 4,343 days;
  • The average number of cutlets per year was about 7.27;
  • The average number of structural months per year was about 41.1;
  • A month contained on average about 104 days assigned to it, but usually stretched almost across the whole year;
  • 97.482% of continuous runs of a month had length one day only;
  • The measured probability that two neighboring chronological days would belong to the same month was only about 2.998%.

In a separate study of “day-year” recurrences, tested across 4,096 self-dates in all directions:

  • Recurring matches of month name + day in month appeared by median within one Pastafari year; 77.56% of matches were in the adjacent year;
  • Recurring matches of cutlet name + day in cutlet were much less predictable: the median was three Pastafari years, but the average was heavily influenced by a very long tail;
  • In the sample, an extreme case appeared where the first future return of “Akkad 3063” was 51,954 Pastafari years away.

These findings are useful for understanding what the calendar tends to do. They do not turn averages into laws: the fact that the average year in the sample had a certain length does not require any given year to be near the average, and a result found in all 4,096 cases is not, by itself, a mathematical proof for every possible input.

How is the calendar calculated?

Behind the date is a discrete and precisely defined algorithm. It takes the day of working and the queried day, produces a sequence of numbers, passes them through a mechanism of confusion called the rotub, creates gates, chooses the year and cutlet boundaries, selects names, creates months, and finally weaves the days of the months through the year.

The exact details include large numbers, drops, bowls, stirring, seals, and combinatorial selection mechanisms. They are important for implementation and formal testing, but they are not required to understand the meaning of the date, and so they are not spelled out here.

Alongside the algorithm there is a fast engine called Pastafarian Calendar Seer. The Seer is intended to calculate quickly; it is not the authority, and it is not itself a standard implementation of the algorithm at every stage. If its output contradicts the output required by the canon, the Seer is wrong.

About the monster

Within the Pastafari story, the Flying Spaghetti Monster is the being that created the world and most of the things in it. Its body is made of spaghetti and meatballs, and it can fly, pass through ordinary matter, and remain invisible when that is convenient.

The monster can create matter, living beings, celestial bodies, and very complex mechanisms. This ability does not require it to prepare a tidy plan before starting work. Often it starts with one thing, moves to another, discovers that the first needs repair, and decides whether to fix it. Sometimes it does fix it.

This image — a being built layer by layer, with repairs, exceptions, and rules left in place — is also the literary background of the calendar. Unlike the story, the calculation itself is not improvised: when the inputs are given, the algorithm returns one defined answer.

The Pastafari story is much broader than the calendar and includes, among other things, pirates, carbohydrates, prayer, “I really prefer not to,” and other traditions.

What is canonical and what is only explanation?

This page is a reference page. It does not create a new rule just because a sentence appears here.

The canonical content is determined by the canonical corpus and the rules adopted within it. Canonical editions of the scripture do not have an automatic hierarchy over one another, and software implementation is not made authoritative merely because it is fast or widespread.

A standard implementation performs the canonical algorithm at every stage. A computational study may describe a phenomenon, find a pattern, or prove a theorem about the algorithm; when it is based on a sample and not a full proof, that fact is stated explicitly.

In brief

The Pastafari calendar is a non-intuitive calendar in which the date depends both on the day being asked about and on the day from which the question is asked.

Year 5000 is built around the day of working. Cutlets are continuous segments. Months are groups of days that can be woven together. There are no canonical weeks. The local day changes according to Venus, not at midnight.

Behind this unusual structure is a precise algorithm; around it stands the Pastafari story of a spaghetti monster, a being built in layers and made of an improbable quantity of carbohydrates.

When the day of working, the queried day, and the place are given — there is one answer.
