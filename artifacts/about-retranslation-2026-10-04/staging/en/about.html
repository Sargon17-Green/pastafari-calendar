The Pastafari Calendar is a deliberately nonintuitive calendar with an unusual structure: a day's date depends not only on the day being described, but also on the day from which the calculation is made.

It has years, cutlets, and months—but the years are not a fixed length, cutlets and months are not the same kind of division, months can disappear and reappear throughout the year, and there are no canonical weeks.

This page explains what the calendar is, how to read a date, and what its structure means in practice. The mathematical and cryptographic implementation details are in the technical documentation, but they are not required to understand the calendar.

What does a Pastafari date look like?

A Pastafari date has exactly five parts:

  1. Year from the Creation of the World;
  2. Cutlet name;
  3. Day in the cutlet;
  4. Month name;
  5. Day in the month.

In other words, a complete date tells you which year the day is in, which cutlet it is in and where it falls within that cutlet, and which month it belongs to and which occurrence it is in that month.

The day of working, the observer's location, and other technical details may be essential to calculating the date, but they are not a sixth, seventh, or eighth part of the date itself.

The way the date is displayed does not change the number of parts, either. If the site prints the five values on three lines, there are still five values; a line break is a typographic act, not the birth of a new calendar field.

Why can the same day have a different date?

Every calculation involves two days:

  • Day of working — the day from which the calculation is made;
  • Queried day — the day whose date you want to know.

If the day of working is denoted by c and the queried day by t, the date is F(c,t), not F(t).

So the same chronological day can receive a different Pastafari representation when the day of working changes. The day itself does not move; only its Pastafari description changes.

When c=t, the day is always in Year 5000. Year 5000 is not a fixed historical period: it is rebuilt in relation to the day of working, so other days around the day of working can belong to it, too.

For example, if a particular day is its own day of working, it is in Year 5000. If the calculation is run again the next day, with that next day as the new day of working, that day is also in Year 5000. These are not two contradictory claims about the same “historical year”; each calculation selects a new base year.

Years numbered above 5000 lie in the future relative to the day of working, and years numbered below 5000 lie in the past. There is also Year 0, followed by years with negative numbers.

How is a year structured?

A Pastafari year can contain between 252 and 5,778 days. Both endpoints are included: a 252-day year is valid, and so is a 5,778-day year; a year of 251 or 5,779 days is not valid under the current canonical rules.

These limits are part of the calendar. The year does not try to match a solar year, a lunar year, a season, a school year, or the patience of someone waiting for the next New Year.

Every year is divided into two different systems:

  • 6–17 cutlets. Each cutlet is a continuous run of days and is at least 42 days long.
  • 3–47 structural months. Each month has 4–123 days assigned to it, but those days do not have to be consecutive.

The calendar has no canonical week system. Rows and columns on the site are only a way of displaying things; the fact that two days appear next to each other does not make them part of the same “Pastafari week.”

What does “woven month” mean?

A cutlet is a sequence: if today is day 250 in a cutlet and tomorrow is still in that cutlet, tomorrow is day 251.

A month works differently. Day 18 in a particular month is the 18th time that month appears during the year. It does not have to come one day after day 17.

Month A — day 14
Month B — day 9
Month A — day 15

A month can therefore be spread across a large part of the year, pass through several cutlets, and be woven among other months. A single cutlet can just as easily contain days belonging to many months.

A month's length is the total number of days belonging to it, not the number of days that passed between its first and last appearances. A 104-day month can therefore span thousands of chronological days.

Why does day 15 come after day 14?

Because 15 is the next whole number after 14. The fact that a day from another month came between the two appearances does not change the count: the first month has already appeared 14 times; its next appearance is the 15th, so its number is 15.

Similarly, if four days from other months appear after day 15 of that month, the next appearance of the original month is still its day 16. The four intervening days count chronologically in the calendar, but they do not belong to that month and so do not increase its day count. That is exactly why “the next day in the month” and “tomorrow” are two different concepts.

This also means the end of a month is not necessarily close in time. A month can be on day 119 out of 120, and its day 120 may still appear much later.

Cutlet and month names

The calendar has 17 cutlet names and 47 month names. Within a year, a name does not appear twice in the same family. Not every name has to appear every year: a year may use only part of the catalog.

The lists show only the names. Clicking a name or the disclosure icon beside it shows its precise meaning and relevant canonical notes. The name is not a code attached to the unit, and it is not a prediction of what will happen in it.

When does the day change?

A local Pastafari day does not change at midnight. Its boundary is determined by the lower transit of the center of Venus across the local meridian: the moment when the center of Venus crosses the observer's local meridian on the side below the horizon.

That is why the observer's location matters. Two people in different places can, at the same physical moment, be assigned to different local Pastafari days. When you move to another city, you do not keep using the day boundary for the previous city.

The boundary depends on calculating Venus's orbit, not on whether the observer can actually see it. Clouds, walls, or the fact that Venus is below the horizon do not stop its orbit.

The calendar's discrete algorithm is precisely defined: the same inputs, processed by the same rules, return the same result. By contrast, converting a physical moment to a Pastafari day currently uses an astronomical model in the implementation; the astronomical numerical profile itself is not yet a closed canonical part.

The calendar's outstanding merits

  • A date you can recalculate again and again: The same day in the past can have a different Pastafari date tomorrow because the day of working has moved forward, too. There is no need to settle for an old date that stays useful over time.
  • Spacious years: A year can reach 5,778 days, so anyone waiting for the next year may face a much longer wait than usual.
  • Months that demand attention: Knowing that today is day 119 in a month does not mean its day 120 will fall tomorrow, next week, or even soon. You have to calculate when it comes next.
  • Geographic sensitivity: The same moment can belong to different local Pastafari days in different places. A trip to another city therefore adds one more detail worth remembering when making plans.
  • Printed calendars do not grow complacent: A calendar prepared in advance may stop representing the correct calculation once the day of working changes, so there is no danger of someone relying on the same sheet of paper for years.
  • Useful work for computing power: Instead of settling for a simple table that can be understood at a glance, the calendar gives a computer the chance to perform an actual calculation whenever someone wants an answer.
  • It has days: The calendar deals in days. This is a feature it shares with every calendar, and it ensures the user will not have to manage a calendar with no days in it.
  • The days appear in order: An earlier day comes before a later day. This is a fundamental achievement of calendars, and in this case it is available at no extra charge.
  • You can use it to specify dates: The calendar lets you assign a calendar description to a day. Admittedly, this is exactly what calendars are meant to do, but there is no reason not to mention a useful feature when it is present.

And all of the above comes together in a single calendar, as happens when several features belong to the same calendar.

What does this mean in practice?

A printed calendar ages badly. Changing the day of working can change the boundaries of years, cutlets, and months. An almanac calculated today is not necessarily the right almanac tomorrow.

An event remains the same event. A meeting, birth, or historical event should be anchored to a stable chronological day or moment; it can later be displayed with a Pastafari date based on the day of working and the observer's location. Changing the label does not move the event in time.

A Pastafari birthday does not follow a “once a year” rule. To find the next occurrence of the same month name and day in the month—or the same cutlet name and day in the cutlet—you have to search the calendar. A nearby year may not contain the required name at all, or may contain a unit too short to reach the desired day number.

An all-day event does not necessarily run from midnight to midnight. If the event is defined by a local Pastafari day, its boundary is the local Venus boundary. A naive export to a civil calendar as a midnight-to-midnight event may change its meaning.

Foundation Day and Tablets Day

The calendar has two important fixed anchors:

Foundation Day
December 22, 41,222 BCE, in the proleptic Gregorian calendar.
Tablets Day
June 15, 763 BCE, in the proleptic Julian calendar, which is June 7, 763 BCE, in the proleptic Gregorian calendar.

The distance between them is 14,777,149 days. Foundation Day is a computational anchor; it is not “the first day of time,” and the calendar continues before it, too.

Tablets Day is traditionally associated with the giving of the tablets and, in the historical calculation, is identified with the solar eclipse day in the time of Bur-Sagale. Both anchors remain fixed even when the day of working changes.

A few simple mathematical facts

There are 47 month names, and every month can reach day 123 at most. Therefore, there are 5,781 possible combinations of the form “month name + day in the month.”

A year can contain at most 5,778 days, and each day realizes one such combination. Therefore, at least three of the possible combinations are missing in every year. Even the longest possible year does not have enough days to realize them all.

When the day of working and the year are known, a complete pair of “cutlet name + day in the cutlet” or “month name + day in the month” points to at most one day within that year. So a complete Pastafari date, together with the day of working, identifies the queried day unambiguously.

By contrast, without knowing the day of working, a complete Pastafari date does not necessarily identify a unique distance along the timeline on its own. The same five-part date can appear in different calculation contexts.

What have computational studies found about the calendar?

In addition to checking the rules themselves, large computational studies have been conducted on the calendar. The following figures are empirical findings from a sample, not canonical rules.

In a structural atlas built from 4,096 days of working and examining 86,016 year structures:

  • The mean year length in the sample was about 4,275 days, and the median was 4,343 days;
  • The mean number of cutlets per year was about 7.27;
  • The mean number of structural months was about 41.1;
  • A month had about 104 days assigned to it on average, but usually stretched across almost the entire year;
  • 97.482% of the continuous runs of a month were just one day long;
  • The measured chance that two consecutive chronological days would belong to the same month was only about 2.998%.

In a separate study of “day-year” recurrences, 4,096 self-dates were examined in every direction:

  • A recurring match of month name + day in month appeared at the median after only one Pastafari year; 77.56% of matches were in the adjacent year;
  • A recurring match of cutlet name + day in cutlet was much less predictable: the median was three Pastafari years, but the mean was affected by a very long tail;
  • The sample included an extreme case in which the first future recurrence of “Akkad 3063” was 51,954 Pastafari years away.

These findings are useful for understanding what the calendar tends to do. They do not turn an average into a rule: the fact that the sample's average year had a certain length does not require any particular year to be close to the average, and a result found in all 4,096 cases is not, on its own, a mathematical proof for every possible input.

How is the calendar calculated?

Behind the date is a precisely defined discrete algorithm. It produces a sequence of counts from the day of working and the queried day, passes them through the mixing mechanism called the Sauce, creates gates, selects the year and cutlet boundaries, chooses names, creates months, and finally weaves the month days throughout the year.

The precise details include large numbers, drops, bowls, stirs, seals, and combinatorial selection mechanisms. They matter for implementation and formal verification, but are not needed to understand what the date means, so they are not detailed here.

Alongside the algorithm is a fast engine called Pastafarian Calendar Seer. The Seer is designed for speed; it is not the authority, and it is not itself a standard implementation of every step of the algorithm. If its result conflicts with the result required by the canon, the Seer is the one that is wrong.

About the Flying Spaghetti Monster

In the Pastafarian story, the Flying Spaghetti Monster is the being who created the world and most things in it. Its body is made of noodles and meatballs, and it can fly, pass through ordinary matter, and remain invisible whenever that suits it.

The Flying Spaghetti Monster can create matter, living creatures, celestial bodies, and very complex mechanisms. This ability does not require it to prepare a detailed plan before starting work. Often it starts on one thing, moves on to another, discovers that the first needs fixing, and decides whether to fix it. Sometimes it does fix it.

This image—a creation built layer upon layer, with repairs, exceptions, and rules left in place—is also the literary backdrop for the calendar. Unlike the story, the calculation itself is not improvised: when the inputs are given, the algorithm returns one defined answer.

The Pastafarian story is much broader than the calendar and includes, among other things, pirates, carbohydrates, prayer, “I’d Really Rather You Didn’t,” and other traditions.

What is canonical, and what is only explanation?

This page is an explanatory page. It does not create a new rule just because a sentence appears here.

Canonical content is determined by the canonical corpus and the rules adopted in it. Canonical editions of the Scroll do not automatically rank above one another, and a software implementation does not become an authority just because it is fast or widely used.

A standard implementation performs every step of the canonical algorithm. A computational study can describe a phenomenon, find a pattern, or prove a theorem about the algorithm; when something is a sample finding rather than a proven rule, that is stated explicitly.

In brief

The Pastafari Calendar is a nonintuitive calendar in which a date depends both on the day being asked about and on the day from which it is asked.

Year 5000 is built around the day of working. Cutlets are continuous runs. Months are groups of days that can be woven together. There are no canonical weeks. The local day changes according to Venus, not at midnight.

Behind the unusual structure is a precise algorithm; around it is a Pastafarian story about a spaghetti monster, a creation piled up in layers, and an unreasonable amount of carbohydrates.

When the day of working, the queried day, and the location context are given, there is one answer.
