The Pastafari Calendar is a deliberately unintuitive calendar with an intentionally unusual structure: the date of a day does not depend only on the day it is meant to describe, but also on the day from which the calculation is made.

It has years, cutlets, and months — but the years are not of fixed length, the cutlets and months are not the same kind of division, the months can disappear and return throughout the year, and there are no canonical weeks.

This page is meant to explain what the calendar is, how a date is read, and what the practical meaning of its structure is. The mathematical and cryptographic implementation details exist in the technical documentation, but they are not a requirement for understanding the calendar.

What does a Pastafari date look like?

A Pastafari date has exactly five parts:

  1. Year from the Creation of the World;
  2. Cutlet name;
  3. Day in the cutlet;
  4. Month name;
  5. Day in the month.

In other words, a full date tells us in which year the day is found, in which cutlet it lies and where it stands within it, and which month it belongs to and how many times it has appeared in that month.

The day of working, the observer’s location, and other technical details may be essential to calculate the date, but they are not a sixth, seventh, or eighth part of the date itself.

Even the presentation does not change the number of parts. If the site prints the five pieces across three lines, it is still five pieces; a line break is typographic behavior, not the birth of a new calendrical field.

Why can the same day get a different date?

In every calculation there are two days:

  • Day of working — the day from which the calculation is performed;
  • Queried day — the day whose date you want to know.

If we mark the day of working as c and the queried day as t, the date is F(c,t), not F(t).

Therefore, the same chronological day can receive a different Pastafari representation when the day of working changes. The day itself does not move; only its Pastafari description changes.

When c=t, the day is always in year 5000. Year 5000 is not a fixed historical period: it is rebuilt relative to the day of working, so additional days around the day of working can also belong to it.

For example: if a certain day is the day of working for itself, it is in year 5000. If the next day is recalculated when the next day has become the new day of working, that next day is also in year 5000. There is no contradiction here about the same “historical year”; each calculation chooses a new base year.

Years numbered above 5000 are in the future relative to the day of working, and years numbered below 5000 are in the past. There is also year 0, followed by years with negative numbers.

How is a year built?

A Pastafari year can contain between 252 and 5,778 days. Both endpoints are included: a 252-day year is valid, and so is a 5,778-day year; a 251-day or 5,779-day year is not valid under the current canonical rules.

These limits are part of the calendar. The year does not try to match the solar year, lunar year, season, school year, or the patience of anyone waiting for the next New Year.

Each year is divided into two different systems:

  • 6–17 cutlets. Each cutlet is a continuous section of days, with a length of at least 42 days.
  • 3–47 structural months. Each month has 4–123 days assigned to it, but those days do not have to be contiguous.

There is no canonical week system in the calendar. The rows and columns that appear on the site are only a display device; the fact that two days are shown next to each other does not make them “the same Pastafari week.”

What does “woven month” mean?

A cutlet is a sequence: if today is day 250 in the cutlet and tomorrow is still in the same cutlet, tomorrow is day 251.

A month works differently. Day 18 of a certain month is the 18th time that this month appears during the year. It does not have to come one day after day 17.

Month A — day 14
Month B — day 9
Month A — day 15

Therefore, a month can be spread across a large part of the year, pass through several cutlets, and be woven through other months. One cutlet can, at the same time, contain days belonging to many months.

The length of a month is the total number of days assigned to it, not the number of days that have elapsed between its most recent and next appearance. A month of 104 days can therefore stretch chronologically across thousands of days.

Why is day 15 after day 14?

Because 15 is the next whole number after 14. The fact that another month’s day has appeared between the two occurrences does not change the count: the first month already had 14 appearances; its next appearance is the 15th, so it is numbered 15.

In the same way, if four days from other months appear after day 15 of that month, the next appearance of the original month will still be day 16 of that month. Those four intervening days are counted chronologically in the calendar, but they do not belong to that month and therefore do not increase its day counter for itself. This is exactly why “the next day in the month” and “tomorrow” are two different concepts.

From this it also follows that the end of a month is not necessarily close in time. A month can be on day 119 out of 120, and yet day 120 of that month may appear much later.

Cutlet and month names

The calendar has 17 cutlet names and 47 month names. Within a year, a name does not repeat twice in the same family. Not every name has to appear in every year: a year may use only part of the catalog.

The lists show the names only. Clicking a name or the opening symbol next to it shows the exact meaning and relevant canonical notes. The name is not a code for the unit or a prediction of what will happen during it.

When does the day change?

A local Pastafari day does not change at midnight. Its boundary is defined by the lower meridian transit of Venus at the observer’s location: the moment when the center of Venus passes over the observer’s meridian on the side below the horizon.

Therefore, the observer’s location matters. Two people in different places can, at the same physical moment, belong to two different local Pastafari days. When moving to another city, one does not continue using the previous city’s day boundary.

The boundary depends on the computed orbit of Venus, not on the observer actually seeing it. Clouds, walls, or the fact that Venus is below the horizon do not stop its motion.

The calendar’s discrete algorithm is defined precisely: the same inputs, under the same rules, always return the same result. By contrast, the conversion from a physical moment to a Pastafari day currently uses the astronomical model of the implementation; the specific astronomical model has not yet been finalized as part of the canon.

The calendar’s notable strengths

  • A date that can be calculated again and again: the same day from the past can receive a different Pastafari date tomorrow because the day of working has also advanced. There is no need to settle for an old date that remains “useful” over time.
  • Long years: a year can reach 5,778 days, so someone waiting for the next year may find themselves waiting for much longer than usual.
  • Months that demand attention: knowing that the day is day 119 in a month does not mean that day 120 will occur tomorrow, next week, or even soon. The next date must be calculated.
  • Geographic sensitivity: the same instant can belong to different local Pastafari days in different places. A trip to another city therefore adds another detail worth remembering when arranging plans.
  • Printed calendars do not become complacent: a calendar prepared in advance can stop representing the correct calculation after the day of working changes, so there is no danger of someone relying on the same sheet of paper for years.
  • Putting computing power to work: instead of settling for a simple table that can be understood at a glance, the calendar gives the computer an opportunity to perform a real calculation every time an answer is needed.
  • It contains days: the calendar deals in days. This is a trait it shares with every calendar in general, and it ensures that the user is not forced to manage a calendar with no days in it.
  • The days appear in order: an earlier day comes before a later day. This is a basic achievement of calendars, and in this case it is available without extra charge.
  • It can be used to specify dates: the calendar allows a day to be assigned a calendrical description. This is exactly what calendars are for, but there is no reason not to point out a useful feature when it exists.

All of these features are available together in a single calendar.

What does this mean in practice?

A printed calendar becomes obsolete. A change in the day of working can change the boundaries of the years, cutlets, and months. A calendar book that is correct today is not necessarily the correct calendar book tomorrow.

An event remains the same event. A meeting, a birth, or a historical event should be anchored to a stable chronological day or moment; only afterward can a Pastafari date be displayed according to the day of working and observer location. Changing the label does not move the event in time.

A Pastafari birthday is not “once a year” by default. To find the next appearance of the same month name and day in month — or the same cutlet name and day in cutlet — one must search the calendar. A neighboring year may contain no instance of the needed name at all, or may contain a unit too short to reach the required day number.

An all-day event is not necessarily from midnight to midnight. If the event is defined by a local Pastafari day, its boundary is the local Venus boundary. A naïve export to a civil diary as a midnight-to-midnight event can change its meaning.

Foundation Day and Tablet Day

The calendar has two important fixed anchors:

Foundation Day
22 December 41,222 BCE in the Gregorian calendar extended backward.
Tablet Day
15 June 763 BCE in the Julian calendar extended backward, which is 7 June 763 BCE in the Gregorian calendar extended backward.

The distance between them is 14,777,149 days. Foundation Day is a computational anchor; it is not “the first day of time,” and the calendar continues even before it.

Tablet Day is linked to the tradition of the transmission of the tablets and the historical calculation associated with the day of the eclipse in the Bor-Sagelle period. Both anchors remain fixed even when the day of working changes.

A few simple mathematical facts

There are 47 month names, and each month can reach at most day 123. Therefore there are 5,781 possible combinations of the form “month name + day in month.”

A year can contain at most 5,778 days, and every day realizes one of these combinations. Therefore in every year at least three of the possible combinations are missing. There are not enough days even in the longest year to realize all of them.

When the day of working and the year are known, a complete pair of “cutlet name + day in cutlet” or “month name + day in month” points to at most one day within that year. Therefore a complete Pastafari date, together with the day of working, identifies the queried day unambiguously.

On the other hand, without knowing the day of working, a complete Pastafari date is not necessarily enough to identify a single distance on the timeline by itself. The same five-part combination can occur in different calculation contexts.

What have computational studies found about the calendar?

In addition to testing the rules themselves, large computational studies have been performed on the calendar. The following are empirical findings from a sample, not canonical laws.

In an atlas of structures built from 4,096 days of working and 86,016 year structures tested:

  • The average year length in the sample was about 4,275 days, and the median was 4,343 days;
  • The average number of cutlets per year was about 7.27;
  • The average number of structural months per year was about 41.1;
  • A month contained on average about 104 days assigned to it, but usually stretched almost across the whole year;
  • 97.482% of continuous segments of a month were exactly one day long;
  • The measured probability that two adjacent chronological days would belong to the same month was only about 2.998%.

In a separate study of “day-year” recurrences, 4,096 self-dates were tested in all directions:

  • The median time to a repeated match of month name + day in month was only one Pastafari year; 77.56% of matches occurred in the neighboring year;
  • Repeated matching of cutlet name + day in cutlet was far less predictable: the median time to a match was three Pastafari years, but the average was influenced by a very long tail;
  • In the sample, an extreme case appeared in which the first future return of “Akkad 3063” was 51,954 Pastafari years away.

These findings are useful for understanding what the calendar tends to do. They do not make an average into a law: the fact that the average year in the sample had a certain length does not require any particular year to be near the average, and a result found in all 4,096 cases is not, by itself, a mathematical proof for all possible inputs.

How is the calendar calculated?

Behind the date is a discrete algorithm defined exactly. It takes the day of working and the queried day, produces a sequence of numbers, passes them through a scrambling mechanism called the Rotb, creates gates, selects the year and cutlet boundaries, chooses names, creates months, and finally weaves the month days through the year.

The exact details include large numbers, drops, bowls, stirring, seals, and combinatorial selection mechanisms. They are important for implementation and formal checking, but they are not required to understand the meaning of the date, so they are not detailed here.

Alongside the algorithm there is a fast engine called Pastafarian Calendar Seer. The Seer is meant to calculate quickly; it is not an authority, and it is not itself a canonical implementation of the algorithm in every detail. If its result conflicts with the result required by the canon, the Seer is wrong.

About the Monster

In the Pastafari story, the Flying Spaghetti Monster is the being that created the world and most of the things in it. Its body is made of spaghetti and meatballs, and it is capable of flying, passing through ordinary matter, and remaining invisible when that is convenient.

The Monster can create matter, living creatures, celestial bodies, and highly complex mechanisms. This ability does not require it to prepare a tidy plan before beginning work. Often it starts with one thing, moves to another, discovers that the earlier one needs fixing, and decides whether to fix it. Sometimes it does.

This image — a creation built layer by layer, with repairs, exceptions, and rules left in place — is also the literary background of the calendar. Unlike the story, the calculation itself is not improvised: when the inputs are given, the algorithm returns one defined answer.

The Pastafari story is much broader than the calendar and includes pirates, carbohydrates, prayer, “I really would prefer not to,” and other traditions.

What is canonical, and what is only explanation?

This page is a page of explanation. It does not create a new rule merely because a sentence appears on it.

The canonical content is fixed by the canonical corpus and the rules adopted within it. Canonical editions of the scripture do not have an automatic hierarchy over one another, and software implementation is not made authoritative merely because it is fast or common.

A canonical implementation executes the canonical algorithm in all of its stages. A computational study may describe a phenomenon, find a pattern, or prove a theorem about the algorithm; when it is based on a sample and not a general proof, this is stated explicitly.

In brief

The Pastafari Calendar is a deliberately unintuitive calendar in which the date depends both on the day being asked about and on the day from which the question is asked.

Year 5000 is constructed around the day of working. Cutlets are continuous sections. Months are groups of days that can be woven through one another. There are no canonical weeks. The local day changes according to Venus, not at midnight.

Behind the unusual structure is an exact algorithm; around it is the Pastafari story of the Flying Spaghetti Monster, a creation built in layers and with an absurd amount of carbohydrates.

When the day of working, the queried day, and the location are given — there is one answer.
